# JAAL research: Bitcoin address clustering, evidence, and uncertainty

**Status:** interim research report, 2026-09-30  
**Scope:** JAAL only: infer likely common control among observed Bitcoin addresses and preserve uncertainty. BUDDHI and SHASTRA require separate audits after this foundation.  
**Evidence classes:** `Implementation` means repository behavior; `Experiment` means a local controlled run; `Literature` means published or preprint results; none of the three alone establishes real-world identity.

## Executive finding

The present JAAL pipeline is a useful, reproducible *candidate address association* graph. It is not an exact wallet detector. In the synthetic `agency-v2` run, common-input proposals were correct 94.4% of the time as individual address-pair links, but their transitive connected components reached only 79.6% pair precision and 28.8% pair recall. Adding SETU change-output proposals improved pair recall by under one percentage point while lowering pair precision to 58.8%. This confirms the key operational risk: an edge can look locally plausible while its transitive closure makes a very large and incorrect ownership claim.

The strongest immediate research direction is not “replace JAAL with a GNN.” It is to retain evidence-level, time-stamped candidate links; distinguish strong/weak/contradictory evidence; evaluate alternative partition rules against hidden ground truth; and make abstention and unresolved addresses first-class outputs. Then benchmark a contrastive GNN *as a cluster-refinement proposal* against non-neural hierarchical and correlation-clustering baselines. The best new 2026 study found for this task follows that pattern, but its principal labels are heuristic-generated and its authors identify limited independent user ground truth as a limitation.

This problem is not logically impossible. What is generally impossible from an incomplete public transaction/capture dataset is to prove that every address maps to one wallet, one person, or a network peer. A defensible system can estimate same-controller likelihood under stated evidence, say “insufficient evidence,” and estimate whether the likely source is absent from the observed candidate set. It must not silently turn those estimates into identity facts.

## What JAAL currently does

`src/chakravyuh/jaal/cluster.py` emits two candidate-link types. The common-input rule star-links all distinct inputs of a non-coinbase transaction with at least two inputs, after the mixer predicate excludes transactions with at least three equal-valued outputs and at least two inputs. The change rule links the transaction inputs to the output selected by SETU's `change_index`, excluding the same mixer shape. `same_owner_edges()` canonicalizes undirected address pairs and keeps the stronger evidence when two rules propose the same pair. The current constants are 0.36 for common-input and 0.25 for change; 0.36 is imported from a literature full-cluster precision statistic and 0.25 is explicitly an unmeasured placeholder. At default threshold 0.2 both edge types enter clustering.

`components()` applies connected components. The stored graph keeps `SAME_OWNER` as evidence rather than editing address nodes, but the cluster result is still a hard partition for consumers. The current saved association edge schema drops transaction ID and timestamp for these aggregated links. That makes it difficult to reconstruct exactly which event created an edge, apply a point-in-time cut, explain a weak bridge, or reproduce an edge-level audit from the graph alone. This provenance loss is a priority to repair before any production promotion.

### What “exact wallet” and “absent source” can mean

JAAL observes address-level transaction evidence; a Bitcoin “wallet” is not directly serialized on-chain. An entity in this simulator is a generated controller, not necessarily a software wallet, person, beneficial owner, or network peer. Therefore the defensible JAAL output is an evidence-backed probability/association between observed addresses plus an unresolved option, not a claim of exact wallet identity.

An address/controller that never appears in the available capture cannot be recovered from that capture. The system can estimate whether the true controller is represented among candidate entities only if it has an operational definition of the candidate universe and evidence about capture coverage. Absence of a match alone is not proof of absence: it can mean an unseen source, a missing observation, an incorrect JAAL merge/split, or insufficient signal. MAYAJAAL can support controlled tests by withholding known source entities, censoring their transactions/announcements, and retaining the hidden inclusion truth for scoring. The resulting target is *candidate-set coverage* or *unknown-source probability*. It is not a legal-person identification probability. A production decision should permit `unknown / abstain` and should report the coverage regime and calibration interval.

### Uncertainty propagation and promotion gate

Every candidate link must retain its transaction/time provenance and rule or model version. Link confidence must not be displayed as cluster confidence: transitive closure compounds bridge errors. Future BUDDHI evaluation must compare (1) hidden entity ground truth as an oracle upper bound, (2) current JAAL partitions as deployed input, and (3) multiple JAAL operating points or sampled plausible partitions. BUDDHI results should report sensitivity to false merges, false splits, and missing source entities. A single hard partition hides precisely the uncertainty that makes subsequent attribution fragile.

Seed robustness is still unknown. An in-memory second-seed generation attempt at 24,000 payments exceeded a ten-minute single-core budget and was stopped before output. The unchanged 20,000-entity world was important to preserve the original entity/payment ratio; shrinking transaction count alone would not be a clean replication. No result was produced, so this is a runtime limitation, not a model generalization result. See R06 in the experiment journal.

The mixer detector is deliberately simple: `has_equal_outputs`, output count at least 3, input count at least 2. It is a useful shape flag, not a comprehensive CoinJoin classifier. Equal-output CoinJoin shapes can be missed; unequal-output collaborative transactions and PayJoin-like constructions can also invalidate common-input assumptions. Conversely, ordinary transactions may have repeated output amounts. The observed `mixer_rule_true=300` is a count of detected shapes, not detector precision or recall. R03 below now measures it against the simulator's mixed-input ownership label and finds poor agreement.

## Ground truth and its limits

The synthetic `agency-v2` entity table supplies an owner entity for every observed address in this run. The observed universe has 121,294 addresses belonging to 16,740 represented entities. This is unusually valuable for controlled internal comparisons: it supports exact owner-pair labels and partition metrics, including the effect of singleton coverage.

It is not an external proof of Bitcoin wallet ownership. The generator code has now been inspected. It explicitly controls multi-input rate (0.35), collaborative multi-input rate (0.05), address reuse rate (0.30), and round-payment rate (0.18); it creates a true change output from the sender, shuffles output order, and writes that output index plus input-owner IDs into the quarantined chain answer table. The supervised target and some feature families (change reuse, output amount/shape, script continuity, input ownership) are therefore generated by the same simulator. This is not necessarily direct label leakage—the address ownership label is created by the simulator and the observable features are derived through transaction traces—but it is a strong generator-specific inductive bias. A model may learn those simulator policies and score well without transferring to real wallets, protocol diversity, or adversarial behavior. All local metrics are *within-generator* results. Inference code must not read `ground_truth`; research/evaluation code may read it only through a documented, split-aware boundary.

An “entity” also needs an explicit meaning. A wallet software instance, custody service, common private-key controller, beneficial owner, person, and network operator are different targets. The current synthetic entity label answers the generator's controller/entity relation. It does not establish a legal person's identity or that a captured peer is the sender of a blockchain transaction.

## Literature: methods worth distinguishing

### Rules and change-output inference

The common-input ownership heuristic (CIOH) is evidence that input keys were jointly authorized for a spend. It is often useful, but collaborative spends are a structural counterexample: CoinJoin inputs are intentionally from distinct controllers. Avoiding only a recognizable equal-output pattern is insufficient as a universal guarantee.

Möser and Narayanan's *Resurrecting Address Clustering in Bitcoin* studies 26 clustering heuristics on roughly 30 million transactions with known change labels, and combines heuristic signals with a supervised random-forest output classifier. Its important methodological contribution is the effort to reduce false-positive-driven cluster collapse while recovering address links; it does not turn every output-shape rule into ground truth. The reported results are specific to their known-change construction and should not be compared numerically to our entity pair metrics. [Paper and tables](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.05749), [conference PDF](https://fc22.ifca.ai/preproceedings/87.pdf).

Schnoering, Porthaux, and Vazirgiannis (2024) formalize and compare six clustering heuristics, including CoinJoin-filtered CIOH and several change rules based on reuse, round values, and input-selection assumptions. They show why address-reduction ratio and temporal growth are not measures of cluster correctness. Their work also emphasizes that “freshness” is only knowable relative to the history visible at the prediction time. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.00523).

Other useful rule families to benchmark as *proposals*, not facts, include: script/address-type continuity; reused-recipient versus fresh-change asymmetry; round-payment and dust/change amount patterns; optimal-change and input-selection assumptions; wallet transaction fingerprints (script types, sequence/version/locktime, fee policy, output ordering); peel-chain detection; exchange/service consolidation; address reuse and co-spend; and protocol-specific CoinJoin/PayJoin detection. Their validity is wallet-era, protocol, and time dependent. For each rule, preserve the raw supporting transaction and measure link precision, recall, coverage, and cluster-level effects independently.

### Peel-chain evidence and peer-to-peer mempool evidence

Kappos et al. (USENIX Security 2022), *How to Peel a Million*, is particularly relevant because it validates co-spend components and then reasons over chains rather than isolated two-output transactions. Its source data comprise 20,016 transactions and 241 addresses from manually validated service clusters plus supplied CoinJoin transactions; it balances 60 true-positive and 60 false-positive *clusters* for analysis. A 500-tree cluster-level Random Forest with five-fold CV reports 89.2% accuracy and 10% false-negative rate versus an 87.5% accuracy / 20% false-negative BlockSci baseline. A chain-continuity expansion reports 0.02% FDR and expansion factor 124.46, compared with 12.7% FDR for the best of the listed prior change rules. Important limits: the classifier is cluster-level, folds are not a chronological prospective test, the sample is balanced rather than population prevalence, clusters represent services rather than individual users, and the expansion FDR treats untagged outputs as unknown positives. The authors explicitly say motivated feature randomization can defeat the method and it is not definitive evidence. [USENIX paper](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-kappos.pdf).

The project already contains a lightweight peel tracer in `src/chakravyuh/jaal/trace.py`: it selects exactly two-output, non-equal transactions whose smaller output is at most 35% of total, initially treats the larger output as change, then follows that address only when exactly one later transaction spends it and the later transaction is itself a hop. On our synthetic chain answer, the point-output rule proposes 7,899 hops among 10,098 two-output transactions with a true change output; it identifies the exact answer-key change address in 5,002 cases (63.3% precision over proposals, 49.5% recall over true-change two-output transactions). It links 625 successor pairs; 454 (72.6%) have the same synthetic sender, and the selected change address is the ground-truth change address on those same 454. This is better than trusting the isolated output guess but still leaves 171 incorrect links among just 625 links and low coverage. It is a full-dataset retrospective diagnostic; do not treat as prospective performance. This suggests testing Kappos-style chain consistency and competing-path constraints before a generic GNN.

Wang et al. (WWW 2024) show that unconfirmed/mempool history has additional structure: five modified Bitcoin clients at different geographies collected 51.2 million transactions during 2022, including 1.415 million transactions that did not confirm. Their proposed replacement-change and dependency/peel-chain heuristics report reducing the number of co-spend entities by 9.8%; the paper acknowledges sparse validation labels and validates selected merges with WalletExplorer. The 9.8% is cluster-count reduction, not measured ownership precision. Mempools are node-local; conflicting transactions, RBF, and failed transactions can be informative but must not be treated as confirmed spends. This literature is unusually relevant to SIH's network/chain fusion: peer arrival times, `depends`/`spentby`, replacement versions, and confirmation/failure outcomes could enrich a future event graph, but the broadcasting peer is not proved to be the wallet owner or original transaction creator. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.01012).

Schnoering and Vazirgiannis (2023) derive protocol-specific transaction-shape rules from open-source JoinMarket, Wasabi 1.0/1.1/2.0, and Whirlpool implementations, including Whirlpool Tx0. They parse the chain through block 760,000. The paper notes a lack of a comprehensive verified CoinJoin ground-truth set and that rules designed as necessary protocol conditions may miss variants while structurally similar other protocols may trigger them. This is a much stronger starting point for a protocol-aware *candidate* CoinJoin feature than JAAL's current generic “three repeated values” rule. It should be represented as multiple protocol-evidence features, with “unknown collaborative spend” retained as a distinct state. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.12491).

The 2026 peer-reviewed FADE framework adds one-time-change, reused-change, and peel-chain-based change rules around multi-input clustering and builds an entity property graph. The accessible IEEE record supports the design taxonomy, but not a full comparison of labels, splits, calibration, code, or cluster purity; therefore it is a pattern to inspect and reproduce, not quantitative evidence of superior accuracy. [IEEE publication](https://doi.org/10.1109/PerComWorkshops68308.2026.11585206).

### Graph communities are not automatically wallet clusters

Address correspondence graphs can support community analysis, but generic modularity communities (Louvain/Leiden), label propagation, spectral methods, Node2Vec plus clustering, overlapping-community methods, and local diffusion answer a graph-structure question. A Bitcoin transaction-flow community can mix senders, recipients, custodians, and services; it is not synonymous with common key control. Fischer et al. (2021) study Bitcoin address correspondence communities and report improvements from clamping a small number of externally known labels, with temporal graph analysis. This is a semi-supervised/transductive setup around known entities, not evidence that unconstrained community detection identifies owners. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2105.09078).

Community detection is still useful for JAAL as a *candidate grouping* or investigative view, especially with signed/typed evidence and seed labels. Compare it against ground-truth partitions with ARI/NMI and pairwise/B-cubed metrics, but do not force a partition when uncertainty or overlapping control is plausible. Transaction-flow anomaly communities belong in BUDDHI/SHASTRA analysis and should remain distinct from ownership.

### Supervised edge and change prediction

Output-level classifiers use transaction structure and address behavior to propose likely change links. Möser and Narayanan's random forest and Garin et al.'s actor-identification work motivate supervised signals. A supervised model can rank candidate edges using amount ratios, script continuity, address reuse, transaction shape, and historical behavior. The label must have a precise target: “same synthetic entity as the inputs” is different from “the change output.” Mixed-owner inputs make a one-parent label undefined, and labels inferred by the same heuristic as features create circular evaluation.

The local experiment below tests Logistic Regression, Random Forest, ExtraTrees, and LightGBM over 18 output-level features. It shows that supervised change proposals can modestly improve this generator's partition score when combined with CIOH, but this is not yet a fair deployment estimate: split and feature-generation semantics need stricter point-in-time controls, and each transaction's multiple output candidates create correlated rows.

### Learned graph embeddings and cluster refinement

The September 2026 preprint *Refining Heuristic-Based Bitcoin Address Clustering with Graph Neural Networks* is unusually close to our JAAL question. It trains two-layer GCN, GraphSAGE, or GAT models with contrastive learning to reproduce heuristic clusters, then applies agglomerative hierarchical clustering inside each heuristic component to identify late, suspicious merges. It uses sampled graphs around 44k–57k transactions, 491k–1.07m nodes, and 3.7m–15.1m edges, with 15/5 neighbor sampling. The default GAT has 64 hidden and 128 output dimensions, four heads, 250 epochs, and five random-seed repetitions. Its results include NMI/ARI/dendrogram purity against heuristic-derived labels, so the main table measures heuristic consistency, not actual user identity. [Preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.01942), [code](https://github.com/hugoschnoering2/btc-graph-hc), [sampled data](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22239038).

On the reported test graph, the GNN variants improve agreement with heuristic labels over Louvain/Leiden; the best reported values include NMI 0.768 and dendrogram purity 0.789. These figures are not comparable to our owner-labelled pair precision/recall. A separate set of 500 protocol-sampled CoinJoin transactions tests negative-pair robustness: the strongest standard GNN-HAC complete-linkage setup reports 45.8% true-negative rate, while the heuristic baseline is 0%; adding CoinJoin-specific hard negatives improves robustness. This is promising evidence for *explicit heterophily negatives*, and direct evidence that topology similarity alone can merge strangers. Authors identify scarce independent cluster ground truth and scalable hierarchical clustering as limitations. It is an arXiv preprint whose PDF lists ICLR 2026 review status; treat as current research, not settled production evidence.

### Recent empirical warning about full components

Müller et al. (2026), *How Reliable Is the Multi-Input Heuristic?*, evaluate real labeled exchange addresses at large scale (reported 854 million transactions and 1.105 billion distinct input addresses through block 795,357). They report strong address-pair recall under the heuristic but much lower full-cluster precision (0.36 in their abstracted results), demonstrating how component closure changes the error picture. Their exchange labels, coverage, and protocol differ from our synthetic data, and do not directly supply a universal CIOH probability. The result is a warning to measure cluster-level errors and coverage, not a transferable constant. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07414).

## Local JAAL experiment (exploratory, not promotion evidence)

**Inputs and reproducibility.** `research/bitcoin/jaal_lab.py` ran on `agency-v2`; addresses, transactions, and `ground_truth/agency-v2/entities.parquet` hashes are recorded in `research/bitcoin/results/canonical/jaal-results.json`. The experiment sorts candidate-output rows by transaction time, uses the first 60% of the observed time range for fit, 60–80% for validation, and final 20% for test. It evaluates 92,768 fit, 25,691 validation, and 32,374 test output candidates. The `chain_txs.parquet` answer table contains 33,049 transactions and six columns including true input entity IDs and true change index; it records 23,177 `change_address_reuse`, 8,185 no-violation, and 417 `common_input_ownership` cases (plus combinations/other rows). The fit takes under four seconds per model on the local CPU. This is a temporal transaction split, but **not an entity-held-out split**, and results use generated ground truth. Multiple outputs of a transaction share the same timestamp and stay on one side of the cut.

**Current rule partition comparison.** The result's `candidate_link_precision` is edge-proposal occurrence precision: repeated transaction proposals count repeatedly. The saved graph edge table canonicalizes/deduplicates unordered address pairs and keeps the highest-confidence evidence; its evidence-specific edge precision is a different statistic. In the current graph, 2,791 unique change-only edges have 1,776 same-owner links (63.6%). In the raw transaction proposal experiment, 4,834 change links have 3,815 same-owner links (78.9%). The apparent discrepancy is denominator and deduplication policy, not contradictory arithmetic. Future reports must state both grain and denominator.

The learned hybrid's pair F1 gain over CIOH is modest (0.423 to 0.437), from one run and one synthetic generator. LightGBM-only edges are extremely conservative at the chosen validation cutoff: proposal occurrence precision 99.7%, but pair recall is only 0.39% because it adds few links. “Precision@20=1.0” in the output-candidate classifier does not mean 20 correct wallets; it means the top 20 output rows are positive labels at 20.3% candidate-row prevalence. It is only a ranking diagnostic and is not wallet-alert P@20.

The cap-128 ablation rejects eight union attempts and changes pair precision from 58.8% to 61.7%, while recall remains 29.8% and impure component count is effectively unchanged (1,524 to 1,527). This arbitrary size cap is not a solution. Simple fresh-output/script/amount rules sometimes create huge false components (one test variant reaches size 9,496); high candidate-edge precision alone does not protect against transitive amplification. These results support an uncertainty-aware partitioning experiment rather than raising confidence numbers or imposing an arbitrary cap.

## Collaborative-input safeguard experiment (R03)

The current equal-output safeguard was compared against the hidden simulator answer `len(input_entity_ids) > 1`. This target means “the inputs were generated from more than one controller.” It is not a protocol label for CoinJoin: it can include other collaborative constructions and the simulator intentionally inserts such inputs.

On 32,787 observed normalized transactions, the equal-output rule produced TP=32, FP=268, FN=1,631, TN=30,856 against that target (precision 10.7%, recall 1.9%). This is a poor detector of all mixed-owner spends in this synthetic population. It does not show the rule is poor at identifying equal-output CoinJoin specifically; the available ground truth target is broader/different. It does show that the current JAAL set-aside cannot be treated as comprehensive protection against common-input false merges.

I trained Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and LightGBM transaction-level screeners from the first 60% of event time, selected a threshold on 60–80%, and evaluated on the final 20%. The 14 observed-shape features, source hashes, splits, metrics, and probability scores are saved in `research/bitcoin/results/mixer-audit-v3/`. Test mixed-input prevalence was 4.8% over all joined transaction rows. LightGBM reached AP 0.763, ROC-AUC 0.978, Brier 0.058; at the validation-selected cut 0.94 it had 136 TP, 19 FP, and 196 FN over all test rows. On the 2,362 final-period transactions with at least two distinct input addresses, this is 136 TP, 17 FP, 196 FN, 2,013 TN: precision 88.9%, recall 41.0%. The equal-output rule on that same eligible set caught 7/332 mixed spends (2.1% recall) and had 55 false positives (11.3% precision). LightGBM feature gain was dominated by input/output counts and amount-shape statistics, not the equal-output flag. This is a strong simulator result and a clear synthetic-policy-leakage risk, not a production CoinJoin model.

Then I tested only final-period input edges, with no later graph events included, for 11,463 distinct addresses and 332 mixed-input transactions. CIOH on all eligible transactions produced pair P/R/F1 .96485/.88083/.92093. Omitting equal-output-shaped transactions produced .96495/.87112/.91564. Omitting transactions flagged by the LightGBM trained earlier and thresholded on validation produced .97560/.88017/.92543. Adding predicted pairwise cannot-links inside flagged transactions to constrained union rejected six further merges but changed pair F1 slightly down to .92510; it did not add measurable value on this test. Thus the next useful experiment is not to rush cannot-link production, but to validate transaction screening across generator seeds and protocol-specific labels, and compare several signed/correlation clustering algorithms under identical forward splits.

This test intentionally evaluates a *test-window-only* partition, not cumulative graph recovery. Entity identities recur between train and test, so it is temporal rather than entity-disjoint. The constrained union is greedy/order-dependent. A single generator seed cannot establish transfer. The validation cut is not a confidence bound; the class-weighted tree Brier score is not a calibration endorsement. Local runtime was about 2 seconds for model fitting and under a second for partition scoring on CPU. No GPU, install, or production code was involved.

### P2P/network feature ablation

The normalized capture contains 906,560 announcement observations for 32,787 transactions. A separate LightGBM ablation compares the same blockchain-only feature matrix to that matrix plus seven announcement features aggregated strictly over `[first_seen, first_seen + 5 seconds]`: observed announcement count, distinct peer/observer/ASN/country counts, peer-seen count, and within-window propagation spread. This is a five-second-latency prediction task, not instantaneous first-announcement prediction.

On the same chronological split and synthetic mixed-input target, test AP changed from 0.76308 to 0.76504, ROC-AUC from 0.97790 to 0.97789, and Brier from 0.05809 to 0.05604. At validation-selected cuts, test precision/recall moved from 87.7%/41.0% to 86.7%/43.4% (136→144 true positives, 19→22 false positives). The gain is a small recall-for-precision tradeoff, not a material ranking improvement. Network feature gain appears, especially announcement count, ASN/country diversity, and propagation spread, but their presence may reflect this simulator's observer/peer placement. The output artifacts and hashes are in `research/bitcoin/results/network-mixer-ablation/`. Keep P2P observations as separate evidence and timing context; do not interpret them as wallet ownership or transaction origin.

### Experiment audit and limitations

1. The output candidate script defines a positive as: all inputs have one known ground-truth owner, and the candidate output has that same owner. Thus every output of a pure-input transaction is a candidate; mixed-owner input transactions generate all-negative output rows. This is not a manually adjudicated change label.
2. The validation threshold picks the lowest fixed cut among a small grid whose observed precision is at least 99%, given at least 50 predictions. It is not a confidence interval or guaranteed 99% deployment precision. The resulting held-out test top-k rows are still output candidates, not deduplicated entity alerts.
3. The “new_test_addresses” metrics evaluate test-first-seen addresses but use partitions built from links across the entire time range. They therefore are not a strict point-in-time snapshot or a fixed-model forward-only entity split.
4. The “small_input_only” filtering logic in `jaal_lab.py` deserves independent reimplementation before using its metric: it constructs a transaction-ID set from the candidate records, then re-runs CIOH. Current report does not treat it as evidence.
5. `candidate_link_precision` in the result counts duplicate edge proposals; `partition_metrics` uses the union-find partition. These are valid but different estimands. The raw output should be versioned with explicit `proposal_occurrence` and `deduplicated_pair` names before it is used in a paper.
6. `mixer_rule_true` is a count and not a ground-truth confusion matrix. The equal-output predicate needs evaluation against `chain_txs`/generator labels and targeted challenge sets.
7. No model is calibrated as “probability these addresses share real control.” Existing 0.36/0.25 fields are confidence-like UI values with incompatible evidential basis; especially 0.25 is unmeasured. Do not interpret either as posterior probability.

## What graph summaries capture, and what they discard

Degree, in/out degree, PageRank, centrality, component and community membership, counterparty count, fan-in/fan-out, reuse, path length, amount flow, velocity, and local temporal counts compress a neighborhood into interpretable statistics. They let a CPU tree model learn nonlinear combinations of transaction context at low inference cost. They capture whether an address is hub-like, newly active, reused, quickly forwarding value, connected to an IP or ASN, or near known suspicious entities—provided those features are computed as-of the event and labels are not leaked.

Compression discards which exact neighbors and motifs produced a statistic, edge order/type, direction-sensitive paths, rare subgraph shapes, and alternative explanations for the same degree or centrality. A learned representation can retain combinations of neighborhood structure that were not hand-enumerated and can optimize similarity end-to-end. It can also smooth unrelated addresses together because they share a transaction, especially in CoinJoin/PayJoin heterophily. If the synthetic generator's labels depend on the same velocity/fan-out/reuse rules supplied as features, neither a GNN nor a tree provides independent evidence of real-world signal.

For JAAL specifically, the main missing information is likely *link-level evidence context*—which transaction supports a proposed merge, whether it is collaborative, whether a competing change candidate exists, and whether multiple independent events corroborate the same pair—rather than generic centrality. Explicit typed, time-stamped evidence with constrained partition inference should be tested before a general GNN.

## Recommended JAAL research path

### Immediate instrumentation

- Keep every proposed link as an event: canonical endpoints, transaction ID, event time/block, rule/model version, raw feature evidence, score, uncertainty, and a reason. Aggregate only for displays; do not erase event provenance.
- Represent positive, negative, and unknown evidence separately. A CoinJoin detector should create explicit cannot-link evidence among inputs; it should not merely omit a common-input link.
- Replace “one component equals one wallet” in user-facing language with candidate groups and confidence/coverage states. Preserve unresolved addresses as explicit singleton/unknown hypotheses.
- Freeze the current graph and result as baseline; record exact code revision/hash and all inputs. No candidate model may read ground-truth fields at inference.

### Controlled model/logic grid

Use identical chronological snapshots and ground-truth evaluation for: CIOH alone; current change rule; CoinJoin variants (equal-output shape, protocol-aware patterns if available, PayJoin challenges); individual change rules (reused recipient, fresh output, round amount, optimal change, output index/amount ordering, peel-chain continuity); supervised change classifier; graph communities (Leiden/Louvain, label propagation, spectral or diffusion candidates); transitive connected components; confidence-threshold components; complete/average-link constrained agglomeration; and correlation clustering / signed graph formulations with cannot-links. Add address/entity disjoint and temporal forward tests. Track operational coverage as well as precision.

Only after these baselines, prototype the 2026-style contrastive GNN as a *split suggestion* on ambiguous/high-risk components, trained with temporal graph sampling and hard negatives from known collaborative transactions. Compare GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT only if a small pilot indicates value; a full HGT is not warranted for JAAL's address-to-transaction neighborhood without clear multi-relation benefit. Include non-neural pairwise metric learning and graph autoencoder as meaningful controls. Train embeddings without evaluation-period topology/features if the claim is prospective prediction; separate the transductive retrospective task explicitly.

### Evaluation contract

Report pair precision/recall/F1; B³ precision/recall/F1; ARI/NMI; component purity distribution and maximum size; fraction of addresses linked (coverage); false merge and false split counts; precision/recall at *address-pair or entity-alert* K after deduplication; and runtime/memory. Break out results by transaction protocol/typology, address reuse, input/output degree, time, and entity type. Show confidence calibration with reliability diagrams/Brier or log loss only where the score is intended as a probability. Bootstrap at entity or transaction group level rather than treating correlated pairs/outputs as independent.

Use three increasingly hard protocols: (1) chronological event split, features strictly as-of cutoff; (2) entity-disjoint test to detect memorization of recurring synthetic entities; (3) generator-shift test with held-out wallet behavior or a second independently generated seed/configuration. Also report a “not enough evidence” rate and abstention-risk curve. A strong model may correctly abstain on most addresses when only a small portion has enough linkage evidence.

## Calls for JAAL

- **Production today:** retain current evidence graph as a candidate-link baseline, but label the derived output as probabilistic/investigative association. Do not claim exact wallets or rely on 0.36/0.25 as calibrated probabilities.
- **First implementation to improve:** preserve event provenance and add explicit cannot-link / uncertain-link handling; then add a validation-backed change-link scorer only if it beats CIOH on the exact frozen protocol.
- **New screening finding:** the current equal-output rule does not cover synthetic collaborative-input spends (1.9% recall against simulator owner labels). A validation-thresholded LightGBM screen had 88.9% precision / 41.0% recall on eligible final-period multi-input transactions and slightly improved test-window partition pair F1. This is promising enough to keep as a research branch, not enough for production until protocol-grounded labels and independent seeds validate it.
- **First advanced research model:** constrained hierarchical refinement, with a small contrastive GraphSAGE/GAT embedding as an optional proposal generator. Treat the 2026 paper as a promising design pattern, not proof of superiority on our data.
- **Do not do yet:** request a larger GPU, install a large graph stack in the disk-constrained environment, or replace component construction with generic communities. The current laptop has an RTX 4050 with 6 GB VRAM and roughly 14.8 GiB RAM; the study-scale sampled GNN is plausible for a later pilot, but current remaining disk space and absent PyG stack make immediate installation unwise. The most valuable next experiments are CPU-scale split/provenance/partition audits.
- **Meaningful “source absent” claim:** for each observed transaction, calculate whether known ground-truth origin lies in the generated candidate set during offline research. At deployment, calibrate a separate “none of observed candidates”/abstain probability on temporally held-out labeled captures. This can say the data do not support a source assignment; it cannot prove the actual person or peer is absent from all captured or uncaptured reality.
